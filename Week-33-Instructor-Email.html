Teachers,

Job is a heavy week. Nearly everyone in your room has had a Job season, is in one now, or loves someone who is. I’ve gathered three themes I would lean into. Take what helps. Leave the rest.

This goes to everyone, from the eleven-year-olds through Gospel Doctrine. The reading is the same in every room, so each theme has a tap-to-open note with ideas for ages 11–14, 15–18, and adults. You know your class; I don’t.

Hymn 136

“I know that my Redeemer lives. What comfort this sweet sentence gives!”

The manual suggests singing this one this week. Its first line is Job 19:25.

Open the hymn in Gospel Library →

Sit with this

Job said this from the ash heap, before any answer came. What do you know that doesn’t depend on getting an explanation?

Adapt for your class: ages 11–14, 15–18, adults

Ages 11–14

  • They know the song. Read the first verse of Hymn 136 and ask what Job knew, and when he knew it. (Not after the happy ending.)
  • Keep the story simple: Job lost nearly everything and still chose God.
  • Ask: what do you know about Jesus that a hard day can’t take away?

Ages 15–18

  • Job 19 comes from the ash heap, not after the restoration. A testimony that only works when life is good isn’t much of a testimony.
  • Ask what they would still believe on their worst day.
  • Job 13:15 pairs here: “though he slay me, yet will I trust in him.” Trust that doesn’t pretend it doesn’t hurt.

Adults

  • “Redeemer” is goel, the kinsman who buys back what a family has lost (see Go deeper). Job is saying family is coming for him.
  • Job 19:23: he wanted these words written in a book forever. They were.
  • Ask where they have seen the Lord stand up for someone who had run out of days.

The goel

“Redeemer” is a family word

  • In the law of Moses the goel was the nearest kinsman who bought back what a family member had lost: land, freedom, a future (Leviticus 25:25).
  • Same word behind Boaz in Ruth 4, the redeemer who steps in when everything is gone.
  • So when Job says “my Redeemer liveth,” he is claiming a kinsman who will stand up for him at the last day, even if every other court fails him.

Handel’s Messiah

Job’s words at the center of the oratorio

  • Charles Jennens built the Messiah libretto entirely from scripture, and opened Part III, the Resurrection and eternal life, with Job 19:25–26 word for word.
  • For nearly three centuries, Job’s ash-heap testimony has been sung as the hinge of the whole Christian hope.

2
 Theme

Comfort begins with presence, not answers

“So they sat down with him upon the ground seven days and seven nights, and none spake a word unto him: for they saw that his grief was very great.”

Open Job 2:13 in Gospel Library →

The covenant

“Willing to bear one another’s burdens, that they may be light; yea, and are willing to mourn with those that mourn; yea, and comfort those that stand in need of comfort.”

This is what we signed up for at baptism. Notice it doesn’t say “explain those that mourn.”

Open Mosiah 18:8–9 in Gospel Library →

Sit with this

Job’s friends got it right for seven days: they sat with him in silence. Then they started explaining. Why is presence so much harder than explanation?

Adapt for your class: ages 11–14, 15–18, adults

Ages 11–14

  • Make it concrete. Someone shares something hard, and the only allowed response is to sit by them. Practice it like a skill, because it is one.
  • Ask: on your worst day, what would you want a friend to do? Not say. Do.
  • Skip the long speeches of Job 4–25. The silence in 2:13 is the lesson.

Ages 15–18

  • The friends’ failure is familiar: explaining someone instead of sitting with them. Group chats do it every day. Everyone has a take; nobody shows up.
  • Job 16:2: “miserable comforters are ye all.” The only thing worse than grief is grief with a lecture.
  • Ask when someone’s presence meant more than their advice.

Adults

  • Job 42:7 is the surprise: God rebukes the explainers and backs the honest sufferer (see Go deeper).
  • Beware any theology that defends God by accusing the wounded. Comfort first. Explanations later, or never.
  • Ask who in the ward is in an ash-heap season, and what presence would look like this week.

God’s verdict on the friends

The epilogue is the surprise

  • After 35 chapters of confident theology, God speaks the epilogue. To Eliphaz: “ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath” (Job 42:7).
  • The friends said correct-sounding things. Job said raw, honest things. God sides with Job.
  • Honesty with God is safer than polish about God. The book itself blesses lament over explanation.

The latter-day parallel

“Know thou, my son, that all these things shall give thee experience, and shall be for thy good. The Son of Man hath descended below them all. Art thou greater than he?”

Liberty Jail, March 1839. Joseph Smith’s “O God, where art thou?” is Job’s question, and the Lord’s answer is the whirlwind again: perspective, presence, promise. Not an explanation. (The manual points here too.)

The landing

“I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee.”

Job never learns why. He never hears about the scene in chapters 1–2. What he gets instead is God, and it is enough.

Open Job 42:5 in Gospel Library →

Sit with this

Job never got an explanation. He got God. What changes when the answer to suffering is a Person?

Adapt for your class: ages 11–14, 15–18, adults

Ages 11–14

  • God’s answer is a nature tour: lions, ravens, wild goats, the sea, the stars (Job 38–39). If you can, step outside for two minutes and look up.
  • “Where wast thou?” is not a scolding. It is perspective. The God who made all of this is holding my small life too.
  • Let the creation do the talking.

Ages 15–18

  • Job never learns about the opening scene with the accuser. He trusts without the full file. Most of us will too.
  • Pair with D&C 122:8: “The Son of Man hath descended below them all.” However low your lowest is, He is already underneath it.
  • Ask what question they would bring to God, and what it would mean if He answered with Himself.

Adults

  • Read Job 38–40 next to D&C 121–122 (Liberty Jail, March 1839). Two men in hard places, one pattern of answer.
  • Note what God does not do: explain, apologize, or restore first. He reveals. The restoration comes after (Job 42:10).
  • The manual’s caution applies: the opening chapters are poetry about the accuser, not a transcript. Don’t build the class on the wager. Build it on 42:5.

Liberty Jail

March 1839: Job’s question, asked again

  • Joseph Smith wrote from a Missouri jail while the Saints were being driven from the state. Parts of that letter became D&C 121–123.
  • It opens with Job’s cry: “O God, where art thou? And where is the pavilion that covereth thy hiding place?” (D&C 121:1).
  • The Lord’s answer (“peace be unto thy soul… these things shall give thee experience”) is the whirlwind speech in Restoration language. God answers the man, not the question.
