Teachers —

A few resources as you prepare for Sunday. Same ground rules as always: I’m not telling you what to teach — not in any way. That belongs to you and the Lord, for the class in front of you.

Job is the week people feel in their teeth. Nearly everyone in your room has had a Job season, is in one now, or loves someone who is. I’ve sat with the materials and gathered three themes I personally would lean into — one of them, or all three. Take what helps. Leave the rest.

This goes out to all of you — from the eleven-year-olds up through Gospel Doctrine. The scripture block is the same in every room, so under each theme there’s a tap-to-open note with ideas for ages 11–14, 15–18, and adults: what to lead with, what to ask, and what to cut. You know your class; I don’t.

The manual suggests singing or listening to this hymn this week — Job 19:25 is where its first line comes from.

Open the hymn in Gospel Library →

Sit with this

Job said this from the ash heap — before the ending, before any answer. What do you know that doesn’t depend on getting an explanation?

Adapt for your class — ages 11–14 · 15–18 · adults

Ages 11–14

  • They know the song. Read or sing the first verse of Hymn 136 and ask what Job knew — and when he knew it (hint: not after the happy ending).
  • Keep the story simple: Job lost nearly everything, and still chose God.
  • Ask: “What do you know about Jesus that a hard day can’t take away?”

Ages 15–18

  • Job 19 is spoken from the ash heap, not after the restoration. A testimony that only works when life is good isn’t testimony yet.
  • Ask: “What would you still believe on your worst day?” Let that be quiet for a minute.
  • Job 13:15 pairs here — “though he slay me, yet will I trust in him” — trust that refuses to pretend it doesn’t hurt.

Adults

  • “Redeemer” is goel — the kinsman who buys back what family has lost (see the Go deeper note below). Job is saying: family is coming for me.
  • Job 19:23 — he wanted these words written in a book forever. They were. We’re reading them.
  • Ask where they’ve seen the Lord “stand at the latter day” for someone who ran out of days.

The goel

“Redeemer” is a family word

  • In Israelite law the goel was the nearest kinsman who bought back what a family member had lost — land, freedom, a future (Leviticus 25:25).
  • It’s the word behind Boaz in Ruth 4 — the redeemer who steps in when everything is gone.
  • Why it matters: when Job says “my Redeemer liveth,” he’s claiming a kinsman who will stand up for him at the last day — even if every other court has failed him.

Handel’s Messiah

Job’s words at the center of the oratorio

  • Charles Jennens built the Messiah libretto entirely from scripture, and opened Part III — the Resurrection and eternal life — with Job 19:25–26, word for word.
  • Why it matters: for three centuries, Job’s ash-heap testimony has been sung as the hinge of the whole Christian hope. It was never a footnote.

2
 Theme

Comfort begins with presence, not answers

“So they sat down with him upon the ground seven days and seven nights, and none spake a word unto him: for they saw that his grief was very great.”

Open Job 2:13 in Gospel Library →

The covenant

“Willing to bear one another’s burdens, that they may be light; yea, and are willing to mourn with those that mourn; yea, and comfort those that stand in need of comfort.”

This is what we signed up for at baptism. Notice what it doesn’t say: explain those that mourn, correct those that mourn.

Open Mosiah 18:8–9 in Gospel Library →

Sit with this

Job’s friends got it right for seven days — they sat with him in silence. Then they started explaining. Why is presence so much harder than explanation?

Adapt for your class — ages 11–14 · 15–18 · adults

Ages 11–14

  • Make it concrete: someone shares something hard; the only allowed response is to sit next to them. Practice it like a skill, because it is one.
  • Ask: “On your worst day, what would you want a friend to do — not say?”
  • Skip the long speeches of Job 4–25 entirely. The silence in 2:13 is the lesson.

Ages 15–18

  • The friends’ failure is familiar: explaining someone instead of sitting with them. Group chats do it daily — everyone has a take, nobody shows up.
  • Job 16:2 — “miserable comforters are ye all.” The only thing worse than grief is grief plus a lecture.
  • Ask: “When did someone’s presence mean more than their advice?”

Adults

  • Job 42:7 is the stunning part — God rebukes the orthodox explainers and vindicates the honest sufferer (see Go deeper).
  • Beware any theology that defends God by accusing the wounded. Comfort first; explanations later — or never.
  • Ask who in the ward is in an ash-heap season right now, and what presence would look like this week.

God’s verdict on the friends

The epilogue nobody expects

  • After 35 chapters of the friends’ confident theology, God speaks the epilogue: to Eliphaz, “ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath” (Job 42:7).
  • The friends said all the correct-sounding things. Job said raw, desperate, honest things. God sides with Job.
  • Why it matters: honesty with God is safer than polish about God. The book itself blesses lament over explanation.

 Theme

The Lord answers from the whirlwind — with Himself

“Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? … When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy?”

Open Job 38:4–7 in Gospel Library →

The latter-day parallel

“Know thou, my son, that all these things shall give thee experience, and shall be for thy good. The Son of Man hath descended below them all. Art thou greater than he?”

Liberty Jail, March 1839 — Joseph Smith’s “O God, where art thou?” is Job’s question, and the Lord’s answer is the whirlwind again: perspective, presence, promise — not an explanation. (The manual points here too.)

The landing

“I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee.”

Job never learns why. He never hears about the accuser’s challenge in chapters 1–2. What he gets instead is God — and it’s enough.

Open Job 42:5 in Gospel Library →

Sit with this

Job never got the why. He got a Who. What changes when the answer to suffering is a Person instead of an explanation?

Adapt for your class — ages 11–14 · 15–18 · adults

Ages 11–14

  • God’s answer is a nature tour — lions, ravens, wild goats, the sea, the stars (Job 38–39). If you can, step outside for two minutes and look up.
  • “Where wast thou?” isn’t a scolding — it’s perspective. The God who made all of this is holding my small life too.
  • Keep it awe, not lecture. Let the creation do the talking.

Ages 15–18

  • Job never learns about the opening scene with the accuser — he trusts without the full file. That’s the bravest faith in the book.
  • Pair with D&C 122:8 — “The Son of Man hath descended below them all.” However low your lowest is, He is already underneath it.
  • Ask: “If you could ask God one question, what would it be — and what if He answered with Himself?”

Adults

  • Read Job 38–40 beside D&C 121–122 (Liberty Jail, March 1839). Two prisoners of suffering; one pattern of answer.
  • Note what God does not do: explain, apologize, or restore first. He reveals. Restoration comes after (Job 42:10).
  • The manual’s caution applies: the opening chapters are poetry about our accuser, not a transcript — so don’t build the class on the wager. Build it on 42:5.

Liberty Jail

March 1839 — Job’s question, asked again

  • Joseph Smith had spent nearly five months in a Missouri dungeon while the Saints were being driven from the state. His letter from jail became D&C 121–123.
  • It opens with Job’s cry: “O God, where art thou? And where is the pavilion that covereth thy hiding place?” (D&C 121:1).
  • Why it matters: the Lord’s answer — “peace be unto thy soul… these things shall give thee experience, and shall be for thy good” — is the whirlwind speech in Restoration language. God answers the man, not the question.

William Blake, 1825

“When the Morning Stars Sang Together”

  • Blake’s Illustrations of the Book of Job (1825) is considered one of his masterpieces. This plate pictures God revealing the creation to Job and his friends — Job 38:7 written around the margins.
  • Why it matters: notice where Job is — small, at the bottom, looking up. That’s the whole of chapters 38–40 in one image.
