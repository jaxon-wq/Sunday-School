The Cyrus Cylinder

A clay decree from Cyrus himself

  • A baked-clay cylinder from Babylon (c. 539 BC), inscribed in Akkadian cuneiform and now in the British Museum.
  • Cyrus announces that he returned captive peoples and restored ruined sanctuaries — the same kind of policy Ezra records for the Jews.
  • Why it matters: it drops Ezra 1 into the real ancient world. Outside the Bible, the same king claims the same return-and-rebuild work — while crediting Marduk, not the Lord.

Learn more at the British Museum →

From the cylinder (Cyrus in the first person):

“I returned to (these) sacred cities… the sanctuaries of which have been ruins for a long time… I (also) gathered all their former inhabitants and returned (to them) their habitations… upon the command of Marduk, the great lord.”

Josephus

A 1st-century Jewish historian on Cyrus

  • Flavius Josephus wrote Antiquities of the Jews in the first century AD — a major outside witness to Jewish history.
  • In Book 11 he retells Cyrus’s decree and claims Cyrus was moved after reading Isaiah’s prophecy about him.
  • Why it matters: another non-Bible voice placing Cyrus next to Isaiah — useful when asking how the Lord stirs people outside the covenant people.

Read Josephus, Antiquities 11.1 (Perseus / Tufts) →

“This was known to Cyrus by his reading the book which Isaiah left behind him of his prophecies… Accordingly, when Cyrus read this, and admired the Divine power, an earnest desire and ambition seized upon him to fulfill what was so written.”

Sanballat’s open letter

The smear campaign, on the page

  • After soft invitations to meet in Ono (Nehemiah 6:2), Sanballat sends an open letter — public, not private — accusing Nehemiah of rebellion and kingship.
  • Treat Nehemiah 6:6–7 like a document dropped on the table: don’t paraphrase the opposition; read the charge.
  • Why it matters: the attack isn’t only a trap meeting — it’s a rumor designed to pull Nehemiah off the wall. Then 6:3 answers it.

First-person memoir texts

Nehemiah and Ezra, in their own voice

  • Parts of Ezra–Nehemiah read like memoir — “I,” “we,” a prayer left in the record — not just later summary.
  • Nehemiah 6:9 names the fear, then asks God to strengthen his hands. Ezra 7:27–28 blesses God for putting the work in a king’s heart and says Ezra was strengthened as God’s hand was on him.
  • Why it matters: strength isn’t the absence of fear. These lines are witnesses from people mid-work.
